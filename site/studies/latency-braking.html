A stopping rule needs a clock.

A 0.25 m nominal stop becomes 0.6825 m when this example includes speed uncertainty, position error and reaction delay. The value of the exercise is making those assumptions inspectable.

The model and the question

Consider a point mass moving toward a stationary wall. An admission rule asks whether its available clearance covers a worst-case stop. It does not issue a command or recover a state that is already too close.

Declared assumptions

  • One-dimensional forward motion; no turns or moving obstacle.
  • Position error is at most ε; speed is at most u = v̂ + δv at observation time.
  • Total delay τ includes state age, computation, transport and actuator reaction.
  • Forward acceleration during the delay is at most A.
  • After the delay, deceleration is at least b > 0 until rest; rest is then held.

Required clearance

D = ε + uτ + ½Aτ² + (u + Aτ)² / (2b)

The terms are position uncertainty, motion during reaction, and braking after reaction. This is familiar constant-acceleration algebra.

A real plant needs valid bounds and a dynamics-to-envelope argument. The new Lean file proves the explicit polynomial bounds, not that a robot follows them.

The literature connection. Recent work studies computation delays in safety filters and escape maneuvers under actuator limits. Those papers motivate this reduced experiment. This study does not reproduce their algorithms or claim their guarantees.

This is a constructed sharp worst-case model. Zero false admissions are expected from the envelope; the grid is a reproducibility check, not an empirical safety rate. It shows the cost of omitting declared terms, not a general performance improvement.

Lean evidence

Four declarations compile: a completed-square braking bound, a reaction-phase bound, their combined polynomial envelope, and the numerical counterexample. Their printed axioms are propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound.

This is not an approved Challenge or a formal proof of the Python/JavaScript implementation.

Executable evidence

Ten focused tests cover the exact witness, independent integration of four varying acceleration intervals, stronger braking, boundary contact, rest, invalid inputs and an understated-delay failure.

A separate AI reviewer reproduced the grid counts using different counting/integration logic. Human review and physical tests remain open.

Where the model can fail

Underestimated data age, sensor error or actuator delay; overestimated braking authority; changing friction; slopes; moving obstacles; turning; and failure to hold rest can invalidate the envelope. If state has already been propagated to the decision time, do not also charge its entire age as future delay. Rejected admission is not an available recovery maneuver.

Not proved here: general bounded-dynamics comparison, runtime refinement, recursive feasibility, probabilistic coverage or physical parameter validity.

Text description of the animation

Speed estimate 1.0 ± 0.1 m/s; position error 0.02 m; delay 0.20 s; acceleration during delay 1 m/s²; braking 2 m/s². The instantaneous rule requires 0.25 m. Including delay and uncertainty requires 0.6825 m. Available clearance is 0.5 m. A point moves through the delay phase and then brakes, crossing the wall before stopping. The bounded admission rule rejects the case; rejection does not repair an already-moving robot. Next steps are validation of bounds, backup feasibility and implementation.

One useful sensitivity question

In the specified example, reducing total delay by 20 ms saves exactly 38.7 mm of required clearance. Reducing the speed-error bound by 0.02 m/s and the position-error bound by 10 mm together saves 26.9 mm. These are hypothetical changes with the other inputs fixed, not measured engineering improvements or a comparison of implementation cost.

That suggests testing the timestamp-to-brake path before adding model complexity. Improving an assumed bound on paper is not evidence that the system improved.
