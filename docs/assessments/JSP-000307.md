## Problem and known solution
Let P(n) be the largest prime factor of n. Erdős and Pomerance (1978) conjectured that P(n) > P(n+1) > P(n+2) holds for infinitely many n. They proved the increasing version, which has an easy elementary proof: take p^{2^k} with k minimal so that P(p^{2^k}+1) > p. Balog (Studia Sci. Math. Hungar. 38 (2001), 45–50, about 6 pages) proved the decreasing version, with ≫ √x such n ≤ x, using analytic and smooth-number machinery.

The JSP catalog entry currently reads "Can three consecutive integers have strictly decreasing largest prime factors?", which is trivially true (13, 14, 15 gives 13 > 7 > 5). There are open correction issues (#14 and PRs #625/#627) to restore the "infinitely many" quantifier. The catalog marks this problem `Eligible to claim: No`.

I found no simpler elementary proof of the decreasing direction. The elementary trick that works for the increasing case does not carry over. The erdosproblems.com forum route, Sungjin Kim's elementary reduction, still needs Maynard–Tao for admissible affine forms as its analytic input. That is how plby's formal proof works.

## What exactly must be proved in Lean
`{n : ℕ | Nat.maxPrimeFac n > Nat.maxPrimeFac (n+1) ∧ Nat.maxPrimeFac (n+1) > Nat.maxPrimeFac (n+2)}.Infinite`.

The statement is now moot for us because it is already formally proved and public.

## Mathlib readiness (exists / missing)
- Exists: `Nat.maxPrimeFac` (Mathlib/Data/Nat/MaxPrimeFac.lean), `Nat.primeFactors`, basic factorization API.
- Missing: Maynard–Tao/bounded gaps, Bombieri–Vinogradov, and the smooth-number estimates Balog uses. plby built a bundled `BoundedGaps` development outside Mathlib to fill this gap. Rebuilding it would take many weeks.

## Proof plan
Not recommended. If we did it anyway, we would have two options. One is to replicate the Kim reduction plus Maynard–Tao for affine forms, which plby already did. The other is to formalize Balog's sieve and smooth-number argument. Both are XL. No finite computation would help, since the statement is about infinitely many n.

## Risks and open questions
- Priority is already taken by plby/lean-proofs (2026-08-17), well before any work we could start now.
- The catalog statement is ambiguous (existence vs. infinitude), and the problem is marked not eligible to claim.
- The maintainers' handling of the many competing PRs is unresolved, but none of that would favor a new entrant.
