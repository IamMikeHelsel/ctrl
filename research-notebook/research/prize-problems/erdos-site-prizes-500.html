$500
If $A\subseteq \{1,\ldots,N\}$ with $\lvert A\rvert=n$ is such that the subset sums $\sum_{a\in S}a$ are distinct for all $S\subseteq A$ then\[N \gg 2^{n}.\]
#1: [Er56][Er57][Er59][Er61][Er65b][Er69][Er70b][Er70c][Er73][BeEr74,p.619][ErSp74][Er75b][Er77c][Er80,p.97][ErGr80,p.59][Er81][Er82e][Er85c][Er90][Er91][Er92b][Er95,p.165][Er97c][Er98][Va99,1.20]
number theory | additive combinatorics
Erdős called this 'perhaps my first serious problem' (in [Er98] he dates it to 1931). The powers of $2$ show that $2^n$ would be best possible here: this provides an upper bound for the minimal such $N$ of $N\leq 2^{n-1}$. This was improved by Conway and Guy [CoGu68] (see also [Gu82]) to $N\leq 2^{n-2}$ (for all large $n$). The previous best known upper bound of $N\leq 0.22002\cdot 2^n$ was due to Bohman [Bo98].

This was disproved by GPT-6 Astra (see the proof expositions), which proved that, for any $\epsilon>0$, there are arbitrarily large $n$ such that\[N\leq \epsilon 2^n.\]The trivial lower bound is $N \gg 2^{n}/n$, since all $2^n$ distinct subset sums must lie in $[0,Nn)$. Erdős and Moser [Er56] proved\[ N\geq (\tfrac{1}{4}-o(1))\frac{2^n}{\sqrt{n}}.\](In [Er85c] Erdős offered \$100 for any improvement of the constant $1/4$ here.)

A number of improvements of the constant have been given (see [St23] for a history), with the current record $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ first proved in unpublished work of Elkies and Gleason. Two proofs achieving this constant are provided by Dubroff, Fox, and Xu [DFX21], who in fact prove the exact bound $N\geq \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$.

An equivalent formulation is to ask for the maximal size of a set of integers in $[1,x]$ which is dissociated (all subset sums are distinct). If $F(x)$ is the size of such a set then this problem is equivalent to\[F(x) <\log_2x+O(1).\]The GPT construction above proves that in fact, for any constant $C>0$, for infinitely many $x$,\[F(x)>\log_2x+C\]for some absolute constant $c>0$. Conway and Guy (see [Gu82]) conjectured that $F(2^k)=k+2$ for all large $k$, but Erdős [Er80] wrote he had 'no opinion'.

In [Er73] and [ErGr80] the generalisation where $A\subseteq (0,N]$ is a set of real numbers such that the subset sums all differ by at least $1$ is proposed, with the same conjectured bound. (The second proof of [DFX21] applies also to this generalisation.) This generalisation seems to have first appeared in [Gr71].

This problem appears in Erdős' book with Spencer [ErSp74] in the final chapter titled 'The kitchen sink'. As Ruzsa writes in [Ru99] "it is a rich kitchen where such things go to the sink".

The sequence of minimal $N$ for a given $n$ is A276661 in the OEIS.

See also [350].

This is discussed in problem C8 of Guy's collection [Gu04].

Written by Thomas Bloom (last edited 03 Sep 2026)
Disclaimer: This is an exposition written to help mathematicians to quickly understand the main ideas of the proof; it will take time to put it into context, and in particular to properly understand the relation of this proof to existing work in the area, and digest what aspects of this proof are 'new'. I will update this exposition as this becomes clearer (or others may add their own, better informed, expositions).

I will sketch the new construction of GPT, which provides a counterexample. In my efforts to understand this construction it ended up quite far from the original GPT writeup, but is (I believe) essentially the same construction, in slightly different language. This is just a sketch, but hopefully suffices to demonstrate the main ideas at play; I also wanted to highlight the connections with Siegel's lemma and geometry of numbers, which I discovered while writing this exposition and were obscured in the original GPT proof, and which should be fleshed out in the 'final form' of this proof.

Let\[f(n)=\inf\frac{\max A}{2^{n-1}},\]where the infimum is taken over all dissociated sets of positive integers $A$. This problem then asks whether $f(n)\gg 1$, and the current lower bounds have the shape $f(n)\gg n^{-1/2}$. The upper bound $f(n)\leq 1$ is trivial, and the best upper bound previously was $f(n) \leq 0.44004$ for all large $n$, by Bohman [Bo98b].

The proof we sketch here provides a much better upper bound: for any $\epsilon>0$, there are infinitely many $n$ such that $f(n)<\epsilon$. The proof is currently non-quantitative, but just due to the non-effectiveness of the part where we approximate an arbitrary lattice by a primitive lattice. It seems likely that using a suitably effective version of this could prove something like, for infinitely many $n$,\[f(n) \leq n^{-\frac{c}{\log\log n}}\]for some constant $c>0$.

The same construction also likely gives a new lower bound for the strength of Siegel's lemma, so before dissociated sets I'll briefly digress.

Siegel's lemma and dissociated sets

(If you only care about the dissociated sets problem, skip this section.) Siegel's lemma, proved by Siegel [Si29] in 1929, proves that, for any $d\geq 2$, for any non-zero integral vector $a\in\mathbb{Z}^d$, there exists a non-zero $x\in\mathbb{Z}^d$ such that $a\cdot x=0$ and\[\| x\|_{\infty}^{d-1}\ll_d \| a\|_\infty.\](This is actually a special case of the more general Siegel's lemma.) Let $C_d$ be the minimal implicit constant on the right-hand side. Bombieri and Vaaler [BoVa83] proved that $C_d\ll \sqrt{d}$, while Schinzel [Sc02] proved that $C_d\geq 1$. It may have been Schinzel who first observed (as recorded by Aliev [Al08b]) that $C_d$ has a close relationship to dissociated sets.

One direction, that $f(d)\geq C_d^{-1}$, is easy to prove: if $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_d)$ is a vector of a dissociated set of integers, then whenever $a\cdot x=0$ we have $\| x\|_\infty \geq 2$, whence\[2^{d-1}\leq C_d\| a\|_\infty\]and so $f(d)\geq C_d^{-1}$.

The GPT construction goes in the opposite direction (at least for infinitely many $d$): essentially it constructs a sequences of lattices which prove that $C_d$ can get arbitrarily large, and constructs corresponding dissociated sets which show $f(d)$ can get arbitrarily small.

Note that this requires the converse of the simple inequality above; it would be desirable to make this connection more explicit, and prove that $f(d) \asymp C_d^{-1}$ for all $d$. (Some form of the argument below may work.) Those interested in this connection should start with [Al08b] or Section 6 of [AlHe23]. For now though, we put Siegel's lemma to one side and return to dissociated sets.

From $Q$-dissociated to dissociated

The first observation is that it suffices to find a large set which is $Q$-dissociated, which means that if $\sum c_iu_i=0$ and $\lvert c_i\rvert<Q$ for all $i$ then $c_i\equiv 0$. If $U$ is such a set in $[1,Y]$ of size $m+1$, and $Q=2^l$, then we can create a genuinely dissociated set $A\subset [1,X]$ with $X\ll QY$ and $\lvert A\rvert=l(m+1)$ by setting $A=\{1,2,4,\ldots,2^{l-1}\}\cdot U$, so that\[\frac{X}{2^{\lvert A\rvert}}\leq \frac{QY}{2^{lm+l}}= \frac{Y}{Q^{m}}.\]In other words, if we define $f_Q(n)$ to be the minimum of $\frac{\max a_n}{Q^{n-1}}$ over all $Q$-dissociated sequences of positive integers $a_1<\cdots<a_n$, then (whenever $Q=2^l$)\[f(l(n+1))\leq f_Q(n).\]So it suffices to make $f_Q(n)$ arbitrarily small.

$Q$-dissociated sets from lattices

A codimension $1$ primitive lattice is one of the shape $L=u^\perp\cap \mathbb{Z}^d$, where $u$ is a primitive integer vector. The connection to dissociativity is the observation that if $u$ generates such a lattice with $L\cap (-Q,Q)^d=\{0\}$ then the coordinates of $u$ form a $Q$-dissociated set: indeed, if\[\sum c_iu_i=0\]then this means precisely that $c\cdot u=0$ so $c\in L$, whence either $c=0$ or there is some coordinate of $c$ outside $(-Q,Q)$.

So the task is now to find a primitive lattice with a 'small' primitive vector. The study of primitive lattices has a long history (in particular heavily investigated by Cassels and Schmidt). The key fact that we need (see e.g. the results of Horesh and Karasik [HoKa23], who actually prove a stronger equidistribution theorem) is that codimension $1$ primitive lattices, after rescaling, are dense in the space of all codimension $1$ lattices. (In other words, the 'distance' of a primitive lattice from a given lattice can be made arbitrarily small after a suitably large dilation.)

(This is a little vague and perhaps overkill as a result, but suffices for this heuristic sketch; the original GPT argument proved directly what was required going via Smith normal form, but I suspect this is all well-trodden ground in the lattice literature, and I leave further discussion to those with a better understand of lattices.)

Let $V_d\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be the hyperplane given by the equation $\sum x_i=0$, and let $\Lambda\subset V_d$ be a lattice of full rank, such that $\Lambda\cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$. Note that, for large integer $Q$, the lattice $Q\cdot \Lambda\subset V_d$ is a lattice of full rank such that $\Lambda\cap (-Q,Q)^d=\{0\}$. We therefore choose $L_Q$ some primitive lattice arbitrarily 'close' to $Q\cdot \Lambda$. This has the property (perhaps after replacing $Q$ by $(1+o(1))Q$) that $L_Q\cap (-Q,Q)^d=\{0\}$, and so it remains to show that, if $u$ is a primitive integer vector with $L_Q=u^\perp \cap \mathbb{Z}^d$, then $u$ has small entries.

Since $Q\cdot \Lambda\subset V_d$, the unit normal of $L_Q$ converges to the unit normal of $V_d$, that is,\[\frac{u}{\| u\|_2}\to \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(1,\ldots,1).\]On the other hand, the covolume of $L_Q$ is equal to $\| u\|_2$, and converges to $Q^{d-1}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)$. Therefore, if $\Delta=d^{-1/2}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)$, every coordinate of $u$ is equal to $(\Delta+o(1))Q^{d-1}$, where the $o(1)$ term $\to 0$ as $Q\to \infty$.

Combined with the earlier observation, this implies that\[f_Q(d)\leq \Delta+o_{Q\to \infty}(1).\]In summary, if there is a lattice $\Lambda\subset V_d$ with covolume $\sqrt{d}\Delta$, such that $\Lambda \cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$, then\[f_Q(d)\ll \Delta.\]It remains to construct such lattices with arbitrarily small $\Delta$.

Constructing the lattices

We construct lattices via an inductive procedure, for which it is useful to actually construct something slightly stronger. Call a pair $(\Lambda,v)$ admissible if $\Lambda$ is a lattice in $V_d$ of full rank, such that $\Lambda \cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$, and $v\in \mathbb{R}^d\backslash V_d$ is such that whenever $tv\in \Lambda+(-1,1)^d$ we have $\lvert t\rvert<1$. We write $h=\sum_i v_i$ for the 'height' of $v$.

We then set $\Gamma=\Lambda+v\cdot \mathbb{Z}$, which is a lattice of rank $d$, and satisfies\[\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma)=\frac{h}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)=h\Delta.\]The goal is now to construct admissible pairs $(\Lambda,v)$ with $\frac{\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma)}{h}\to 0$.

There are two parts to this: constructing an initial admissible pair, and then iterating this. Both parts are elementary, and where the real novelty of the proof lies, so I'll try to spell this out carefully.

Let $b\geq 3$ be an odd integer. We start by constructing an admissible pair $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^b$. Consider the cyclic shift linear transformation given by $(Tz)_i=z_i+\frac{1}{2}z_{i-1}$ (with the indices interpreted cyclically). Note that this maps $V_b\to V_b$. Let $\Lambda_0=\mathbb{Z}^b\cap V_b$, and $\Lambda_1=T(\Lambda_0)\subset V_b$. Let\[v_1=T(1,0,\ldots,0)=(1,0,\ldots,0,1/2),\]so that $h_1=3/2$.

We claim that $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ is admissible. Let's first show that $\Lambda\cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$. Suppose that $T(z)\in (-1,1)^d$ for some $z\in \Lambda_0$. This means that\[\lvert z_i+\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i-1}\rvert <1\]for all $0\leq i<b$, which implies that $\lvert z_i\rvert <2$ for all $0\leq i<b$. Since all $z_i$ are integers, they must all be in $\{-1,0,1\}$. Now if $z_i=1$ this further implies that $z_{i-1}=-1$, and then $z_{i-2}=1$, and so on. But since this goes round all coordinates cyclically, and there are an odd number of coordinates, we get $z_i=-1$, a contradiction. Therefore $z_i=0$ for all $z$, so $T(z)=0$ as required.

We also need to show that if $tv\in \Lambda+(-1,1)^d$ then $\lvert t\rvert<1$. This is shown by a similar argument: suppose there exists $z'\in \Lambda_0$ such that if $z=z'+t(1,0,\ldots,0)$ then $T(z)\in (-1,1)^b$. All coordinates of $z$, except possibly the first, are integral, so by the argument in the preceding paragraph, coupled with the fact that $z=z'+t(1,0,\ldots,0)$ for some $z'\in V_b$,\[z=(z_0,z_1,\ldots,z_{b-1})=\left(t-\sum_{i\geq 1}z_i, 1,-1,1,-1,\ldots,0,0,\ldots,0\right)\](or the same with the signs flipped).

There are now a number of cases. If $z_i=0$ for $i\geq 1$ then this implies $z_0=t$ and $\lvert t\rvert=\lvert z_0+z_{b-1}/2\rvert<1$ and we are done.

Otherwise, if $z_{b-1}=0$ then $\lvert z_0\rvert=\lvert z_0+\frac{1}{2}z_{b-1}\rvert <1$, but also $\lvert z_1+\frac{1}{2}z_0\rvert<1$ and $z_1=1$ so $z_0<0$, and hence\[z_0=t-\sum_{i\geq 1}z_i\in (-1,0).\]The sum here is a (possibly empty) alternating sum of $\pm 1$, beginning with $+1$, so is either $0$ or $1$, and thus we again have $\lvert t\rvert <1$.

Finally, if $z_{b-1}=-1$ (the only other possibility, since $b$ is odd) then $\sum_{i\geq 1}z_i=0$ and so $z_0=t$. Using the cyclic inequality twice we have $\lvert 1+t/2\rvert<1$, so and so $t<0$, and\[\lvert t-1/2\rvert=\lvert z_0+\tfrac{1}{2}z_{b-1}\rvert <1,\]whence $t>-1/2$ and again $\lvert t\rvert<1$.

This constructs an admissible pair $(\Lambda,v)$ in $\mathbb{R}^b$, with $h=3/2$, and if $\Gamma_1=\Lambda_1+v_1\cdot \mathbb{Z}=M\mathbb{Z}^b$, where $M=I+\frac{1}{2}P$ where $P$ is the cyclic shift $z_i\mapsto z_{i-1}$, and hence\[\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_1)=\lvert \det M\rvert=1+2^{-b}.\]By itself, this has\[\Delta=\frac{1+2^{-b}}{3/2}.\]Taking $b\to \infty$ yields $f(b)\leq (1+o(1))\frac{2}{3}$, which does not even beat Bohman's bound. But this admissible definition is ripe for iteration.

The iteration

Given an admissible pair $(\Lambda_s,v_s)$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, define the admissible pair $(\Lambda_{s+1},v_{s+1})$ in $\mathbb{R}^{db}$ by\[\Lambda_{s+1}=\{(\lambda_0+\beta_0v_s,\ldots,\lambda_{b-1}+\beta_{b-1}v_s) : \lambda_j \in \Lambda_s\textrm{ and }\beta\in \Lambda_1\}\]and\[v_{s+1}=(v_s,0,\ldots,0,v_s/2).\]It is easy to check that $\Lambda_{s+1}$ is a lattice of rank $bd-1$ in $V_{bd}$ (note that the parameterisation is injective, since summing the coordinates in the $j$th block gives $\beta_jh_s$, whence $\beta$ can be recovered as $h_s\neq 0$).

Furthermore, this pair is indeed admissible. Firstly, suppose $y\in \Lambda_{s+1}\cap (-1,1)^{bd}$. Each 'block' of $y$ is in $(\Lambda_s+\beta_iv_s)\cap (-1,1)^{d}$, so by admissibility of $(\Lambda_s,v_s)$ we have $\lvert \beta_i\rvert <1$ for all $i$. Therefore, since $\beta\in \Lambda_1 \cap (-1,1)^b$, by admissibility of $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ we have $\beta=0$. Now each block is in $\Lambda_s\cap (-1,1)^d$, and so by admissibility again vanishes everywhere, so $y=0$.

Similarly, if\[y\in (\Lambda_{s+1}+tv_{s+1})\cap (-1,1)^{bd}\]then the $j$th block looks like $\lambda_j+\gamma_j v_s$ for some $\lambda_j\in \Lambda_s$, where $\gamma=\beta+tv_1$ for some $\beta\in \Lambda_1$. By admissibility of $(\Lambda_s,v_s)$ we have $\lvert \gamma_j\rvert <1$, and so\[\gamma=\beta+tv_1\in (\Lambda_1+tv_1)\cap (-1,1)^b,\]so by admissibility of $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ we have $\lvert t\rvert<1$ as required.


The heights multiply as $h_{s+1}=h_1h_s$, and so $h_s=(3/2)^s$. Finally, since elements of $\Gamma_{s+1}=\Lambda_{s+1}+v_{s+1}\cdot \mathbb{Z}$ have the shape\[(\lambda_0+\beta_0v_s,\ldots,\lambda_{b-1}+\beta_{b-1}v_s)\]where $\lambda_j\in \Lambda_s$ and $\beta\in \Gamma_1$,
the covolumes similarly scale as\[\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{s+1})=\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{1})\left(\frac{h_s}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda_s)\right)^b=\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{1})\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{s})^b.\](To see this, note that in each block of the shape $(\lambda_j+\beta_jv_s)$, one unit in the \(\beta\)-direction contributes perpendicular length \(h_s/\sqrt d\), so the corresponding covolume factor is $(h_s/\sqrt d)\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_s)$.)

Putting all this together, after $s$ iterations, we have (in $d=b^s$ dimensions)\[\Delta\leq \frac{\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_s)}{h_s}= \frac{(1+2^{-b})^{1+b+\cdots+b^{s-1}}}{(3/2)^s}.\]For fixed $s$ the numerator $\to 1$ as $b\to \infty$, and so $\Delta$ can be made arbitrarily small by first choosing $s$ very large and then $b$ very large.

$500
If $G$ is an edge-disjoint union of $n$ copies of $K_n$ then is $\chi(G)=n$?
#19: [Er76b,p.171][Er76c,p.9][Er78,p.29][Er81][Er90][Er92b][Er93,p.341][Er95][Er97c][Er97d][Er97f][Va99,3.57]
graph theory | chromatic number
Conjectured by Erdős, Faber, and Lovász (apparently 'at a party in Boulder, Colarado in September 1972' [Er81]).

Kahn [Ka92] proved that $\chi(G)\leq (1+o(1))n$ (for which Erdős gave him a 'consolation prize' of \$100). Hindman [Hi81] proved the conjecture for $n<10$. Various special cases have been established by Romero and Sáchez-Arroyo [RoSa07], Araujo-Pardo and Vázquez-Ávila [ArVa16], and Alesandroi [Al21].

Kang, Kelly, Kühn, Methuku, and Osthus [KKKMO21] have proved the answer is yes for all sufficiently large $n$.

In [Er97d] Erdős asks how large $\chi(G)$ can be if instead of asking for the copies of $K_n$ to be edge disjoint we only ask for their intersections to be triangle free, or to contain at most one edge.

In [Er93] Erdős and Füredi conjecture the generalisation that if $G$ is the union of $n$ copies of $K_n$, which pairwise intersect in at most $k$ vertices, then $\chi(G)\leq kn$. This has been proved for all sufficiently large $n$ (not depending on $k$) by Kang, Kelly, Kühn, Methuku, and Osthus [KKKMO24]. Furthermore, Horák and Tuza [HoTu90] proved that if $\chi(G) \leq n^{3/2}$ if $G$ is the union of $n$ copies of $K_n$, and hence this conjecture also holds whenever $k\geq\sqrt{n}$.

In [Er78] Erdős asks for the smallest $m_k$ such that there exists an edge-disjoint union of $m_k$ copies of $K_n$ which has chromatic number at least $n+k$ (so that the original question is whether $m_1>n$). He writes 'at the moment I do not even have a plausible conjecture, but perhaps this will not be hard to find'.

Let $f(n)$ be minimal such that there is an intersecting family $\mathcal{F}$ of sets of size $n$ (so $A\cap B\neq\emptyset$ for all $A,B\in \mathcal{F}$) with $\lvert \mathcal{F}\rvert=f(n)$ such that any set $S$ with $\lvert S\rvert \leq n-1$ is disjoint from at least one $A\in \mathcal{F}$.

Is it true that\[f(n) \ll n?\]
#21: [Er81][Er90][Er92b][Er97f]
combinatorics | intersecting family
Conjectured by Erdős and Lovász [ErLo75], who proved that\[\frac{8}{3}n-3\leq f(n) \ll n^{3/2}\log n\]for all $n$. The upper bound was improved by Kahn [Ka92b] to\[f(n) \ll n\log n.\](The upper bound constructions in both cases are formed by taking a random set of lines from a projective plane of order $n-1$, assuming $n-1$ is a prime power.)

This problem was solved by Kahn [Ka94] who proved the upper bound $f(n) \ll n$. The Erdős-Lovász lower bound of $\frac{8}{3}n-O(1)$ has not been improved, and it has been speculated (see e.g. [Ka94]) that the correct answer is $3n+O(1)$.

It is trivial that $f(1)=1$ and $f(2)=3$. The values $f(3)=6$ and $f(4)=9$ were established by Tripathi [Tr14]. Barát and Wanless [BaWa21] proved that $f(5)=13$, and that $13\leq f(6)\leq 18$.

Is there an infinite Sidon set $A\subset \mathbb{N}$ such that\[\lvert A\cap \{1\ldots,N\}\rvert \gg_\epsilon N^{1/2-\epsilon}\]for all $\epsilon>0$?
#39: [Er56][Er61][Er73][Er77c][Er80,p.98][ErGr80,p.48][Er81][Er82e][Er85c][Er91][Er95][Er97c][Va99,1.18]
number theory | sidon sets | additive combinatorics
The trivial greedy construction achieves $\gg N^{1/3}$. The first improvement on this was achieved by Ajtai, Komlós, and Szemerédi [AKS81b], who found an infinite Sidon set with growth rate $\gg (N\log N)^{1/3}$. The current best bound of $\gg N^{\sqrt{2}-1+o(1)}$ is due to Ruzsa [Ru98].

Erdős [Er73] had offered \$25 for any construction which achieves $N^{c}$ for some $c>1/3$. Later he ([Er77c] and [Er80]) offered \$100 for a construction which achieves $\omega(N)N^{1/3}$ for some $\omega(N)\to \infty$.

Erdős proved that for every infinite Sidon set $A$ we have\[\liminf \frac{\lvert A\cap \{1,\ldots,N\}\rvert}{N^{1/2}}=0.\]Erdős and Rényi have constructed, for any $\epsilon>0$, a set $A$ such that\[\lvert A\cap \{1\ldots,N\}\rvert \gg_\epsilon N^{1/2-\epsilon}\]for all large $N$ and $1_A\ast 1_A(n)\ll_\epsilon 1$ for all $n$.

This is discussed in problem C9 of Guy's collection [Gu04].

Written by Thomas Bloom (last edited 03 Sep 2026)
Disclaimer: This is an exposition written to help mathematicians to quickly understand the main ideas of the proof; it will take time to put it into context, and in particular to properly understand the relation of this proof to existing work in the area, and digest what aspects of this proof are 'new'. I will update this exposition as this becomes clearer (or others may add their own, better informed, expositions).

I will sketch the ideas behind GPT's disproof. In fact this proof proves the following stronger fact.

Let $D(n)$ be such that every subgraph on $\leq n$ edges can be made bipartite by deleting at most $D(n)$ edges. There is a constant $c>0$ such that if $G$ is a graph (finite or infinite) such that $D(n) \leq c\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}$, then $G$ has chromatic number $\leq 3$.

(The formalisation just proves this with $\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}$ replaced by some function $f\to \infty$, but the proof sketched below seems to work with this choice.)

By a compactness argument it suffices to prove this just for finite graphs.

Removing short odd cycles

Let $L\geq 2$ and let $B_k=L+\cdots+L^k$. Here we prove that, if $D(n)\leq d$ for every $n\leq B_{d+1}$, then all odd cycles of length $\leq L$ can be removed from $G$ by deleting at most $d$ edges.

Call a set $S$ of odd cycles of length $\leq L$ $k$-rich if every set of $<k$ edges misses some cycle in $S$.

We claim by induction that, for any $k\geq 0$, given any $k$-rich $S$, there is a $k$-rich $S'\subseteq S$ which contains at most $B_k$ edges. The case $k=0$ is trivial. For the inductive step, choose a cycle $C\in S$, and for each edge $e\in C$ let $S_e\subseteq S$ be the set of cycles avoiding $e$. If $S$ is $(k+1)$-rich then each $S_e$ is $k$-rich, and so by the inductive hypothesis there exists a $k$-rich $S_e'\subseteq S_e$ on at most $B_k$ edges, and taking $S'=\{C\}\cup \cup_{e\in C}S_e'$ finishes the proof, since $L+LB_k=B_{k+1}$.

Now apply this with $S$ being the set of all odd cycles of length $\leq L$ in $G$ and $k=d+1$. If $S$ is not $(d+1)$-rich we are done; otherwise there is a graph on at most $B_{d+1}$ edges which is $(d+1)$-rich, but by assumption this graph can be made bipartite by deleting at most $d$ edges, so certainly can't contain any odd cycles after that, a contradiction.

Finding a $3$-colouring

Suppose that $G_N\subseteq G_{N_1}\subseteq\cdots \subseteq G_0$ (with all vertices kept at each stage, just removing edges), such that every edge removed when going from $G_k$ to $G_{k+1}$ has both endpoints in some set $S_k$, where $\lvert S_k\rvert\ll k$. If $G_k$ contains no odd cycles of length at most $L_k=O(k^4)$, for every $k$, and $G_N$ is bipartite, then $G_0$ has a $3$-colouring in which the third colour only appears distance $R_N\asymp N^2$ from $\cup S_i$. (For this heuristic sketch purpose we are being informal with the precise choice of parameters.)

This is proved by induction on $N$. This is trivial when $N=0$. In general, suppose we know it for $N$. Let $B$ be the radius $R_{N+1}$-neighbourhood of $A=\cup_{i<N} S_i$. We first note that $G_N[B]$ is bipartite - if not, then it must contain an odd cycle, and since everything in $B$ is within $O(R_N)$-distance of $A$, which has size $O(N^2)$, it must contain an odd cycle of length $O(N^4)$, which contradicts the choice of $L_N$.

We now consider the corresponding $B_N\subseteq\cdots \subseteq B_0$. By induction, $B=B_0$ has a $3$-colouring in which the third colour only appears distance $R_N$ from $A$. (Note that $B$ itself was the $R_{N+1}$ neighbourhood of $A$, so there is an annulus of width $R_{N+1}-R_N\asymp N$ around $A$ which only receives two colours.)

Now we create another colouring: start with a $2$-colouring of $G_{N+1}$. The graph $G_N[S_N]$ is bipartite (since $S_N\ll N$ and so if it contained an odd cycle it would contain one of length $\leq L_N$). We can therefore produce a $3$-colouring of $G_N$, in which the third colour only appears in $S_N$, just by adding a third colour to one side of the bipartition of $S_N$.

Now we have two $3$-colourings: one of $B$, the $O(N^2)$-neighbourhood of $A=\cup_{i<N}S_i$, such that the third colour never appears in an annulus of width $\asymp N$ around $A$, and another of $G_{N}$, in which the third colour only appears in $S_N$.

Now there is an annulus of width $\asymp N$ around $A$ which receives only two colours from the first colour. Since $\lvert S_N\rvert\ll N$, by the pigeonhole principle there is an annulus of width $4$ which contains no vertices from $S_N$, and hence only two colours from the second colouring also. We can then glue the two colourings across this thin annulus (note that no edge jumps over the annulus), going from the inner colouring to the outer colouring and perhaps re-introducing the third colour at some places along the join. (This means that the inductive hypothesis, that the third colour only appears distance $O(N^2)$ from $\cup_iS_i$, is preserved.)

More precisely, suppose $a$ is the 'inner' colouring and $b$ is the 'outer' colouring. Suppose we have a $4$-layer annulus on which both $a$ and $b$ only use the colours $1$ and $2$, say on layers $t,t+1,t+2,t+3$. Now on layers $\leq t$ use $a$; on layers $\geq t+3$ use $b$. On layer $t+1$ start with colour $a$ and on layer $t+2$ start with colour $b$. The only possible conflict comes from if there is an edge $uv$ in the overlap where $u$ is on layer $t+1$ and $v$ is on layer $t+2$, and $a(u)=b(v)$. These are removed by colouring any $v$ on layers $t+1$ or $t+2$ with the third colour $3$ if $a(v)=2$ and $b(v)=1$.

Completing the proof

We now choose $f(n)\asymp \frac{\log n}{\log\log n}$ such that $f(n)$ is the minimum $k$ such that $n\leq B_{k+1}(L_k)$, where $L_k=O(k^4)$ is chosen as in the previous section. (Since $B_d(L) \ll L^d$, $ B_{k+1}(L_k)$ grows like $k^{O(k)}$ and so $f$ has the growth rate claimed.)

Suppose that $D(n)\leq f(n)$ for all $n$. Start with $G_0=G$, and repeatedly apply the deletion lemma from the second section. Each time we produce some $G_{k+1}$ after deleting $O(k)$ many edges and removing all odd cycles of length at most $L_{k+1}$. If we repeatedly do this until $L_N\geq \lvert G\rvert$ then $G_N$ can contain no odd cycles of any length, so must be bipartite, and we have a sequence as in the previous section.

#90: [Er46b][Er61,p.243][Er75f,p.100][Er81,p.13][Er82e,p.61][Er83c,p.52][Er85,p.2][Er90,p.475][Er94b,p.267][Er95,p.14][Er97c,p.68][Er97e,p.528][Er97f,p.5][Va99,4.67]

The unit distance problem. In [Er94b] Erdős dates this conjecture to 1946. In [Er82e] he offers \$300 for a proof or disproof of the upper bound $n^{1+o(1)}$.

This would be the best possible, as is shown by a set of lattice points. It is easy to show that there are $O(n^{3/2})$ many such pairs. Spencer, Szemerédi, and Trotter [SST84] proved an upper bound of $O(n^{4/3})$. In [Er83c] and [Er85] Erdős offers \$250 for an upper bound of the form $n^{1+o(1)}$.

Part of the difficulty of this problem is explained by a result of Valtr (see [Sz16]), who constructed a metric on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and a set of $n$ points with $\gg n^{4/3}$ unit distance pairs (with respect to this metric). The methods of the upper bound proof of Spencer, Szemerédi, and Trotter [SST84] generalise to include this metric. Therefore to prove an upper bound better than $n^{4/3}$ some special feature of the Euclidean metric must be exploited.

See a survey by Szemerédi [Sz16] for further background and related results.

This has been disproved: there are, for infinitely many $n$, a set $P$ of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that the number of unit distance pairs in $P$ is at least $n^{1+c}$, where $c>0$ is an absolute constant. For further details see this paper by Sawin, and this paper for further remarks.

A formal proof of the upper bound $O(n^{4/3-c})$ for some constant $c>0$ is available here.

See also [92], [96], [605], and [956]. The higher dimensional generalisation is [1085].

$500
Let $f(n)$ be minimal such that any $f(n)$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$, no three on a line, contain $n$ points which form the vertices of a convex $n$-gon. Prove that $f(n)=2^{n-2}+1$.
#107: [Er61,p.245][Er75f,p.106][Er81][Er82e][Er83c][Er95,p.184][Er97c][Er97e][Va99,4.66]
geometry | convex
The Erdős-Klein-Szekeres 'Happy Ending' problem. The problem originated in 1931 when Klein observed that $f(4)=5$. Turán and Makai showed $f(5)=9$. Erdős and Szekeres proved the bounds\[2^{n-2}+1\leq f(n)\leq \binom{2n-4}{n-2}+1.\]([ErSz60] and [ErSz35] respectively). There were several improvements of the upper bound, but all of the form $4^{(1+o(1))n}$, until Suk [Su17] proved\[f(n) \leq 2^{(1+o(1))n}.\]The current best bound is due to Holmsen, Mojarrad, Pach, and Tardos [HMPT20], who prove\[f(n) \leq 2^{n+O(\sqrt{n\log n})}.\]In [Er97e] Erdős clarifies that the \$500 is for a proof, and only offers \$100 for a disproof. Graham [Gr04] offers \$1000 for a proof.

$500
Let the van der Waerden number $W(k)$ be such that whenever $N\geq W(k)$ and $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ is $2$-coloured there must exist a monochromatic $k$-term arithmetic progression. Improve the bounds for $W(k)$ - for example, prove that $W(k)^{1/k}\to \infty$.
#138: [Er57][Er61][Er73][Er74b][Er75b][Er77c][ErGr79][Er80,p.90][ErGr80][Er81][Er97c]
additive combinatorics
When $p$ is prime Berlekamp [Be68] has proved $W(p+1)\geq p2^p$. Gowers [Go01] has proved\[W(k) \leq 2^{2^{2^{2^{2^{k+9}}}}}.\]The best general lower bound is $W(k)\gg 2^k$, due to Kozik and Shabanov [KoSh16].

In [Er81] Erdős further asks whether $W(k+1)/W(k)\to \infty$, or $W(k+1)-W(k)\to \infty$. DeepMind has proved (see the comments) that\[W(k+1)\geq W(k)+k,\]answering the second question.

In [Er80] Erdős asks whether $W(k)/2^k\to \infty$, and offers \$500 for a proof or disproof of $W(k)^{1/k}\to \infty$.

The analogous question for $r\geq 3$ colours was resolved by Fox and Hunter [FoHu26], who proved\[W_3(k)^{1/k}\geq C^{\log_*k}\]for some constant $C>1$.

A formal proof that, for all $r\geq 2$,\[W_r(k)\geq k^{ck\log r}\]for some constant $c>0$, is available here.

$500
Let $A\subset (1,\infty)$ be a countably infinite set such that for all $x\neq y\in A$ and integers $k\geq 1$ we have\[ \lvert kx -y\rvert \geq 1.\]Does this imply that $A$ is sparse? In particular, does this imply that\[\sum_{x\in A}\frac{1}{x\log x}<\infty\]or\[\sum_{\substack{x <n\\ x\in A}}\frac{1}{x}=o(\log n)?\]
#143: [Er61][Er73][Er77c][Er80,p.101][Er92c][Er97c]
primitive sets
Note that if $A$ is a set of integers then the condition implies that $A$ is a primitive set (that is, no element of $A$ is divisible by any other), for which the convergence of $\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n\log n}$ was proved by Erdős [Er35], and the upper bound\[\sum_{\substack{x <n\\ x\in A}}\frac{1}{n}\ll \frac{\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\]was proved by Behrend [Be35]. This $O(\cdot)$ bound was improved to a $o(\cdot)$ bound by Erdős, Sárkőzy, and Szemerédi [ESS67].

In [Er73] and [Er77c] Erdős mentions an unpublished proof of Haight that\[\lim \frac{\lvert A\cap [1,x]\rvert}{x}=0\]holds if the elements of $A$ are independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Over the years Erdős asked for various different quantitative estimates, for example\[\liminf \frac{\lvert A\cap [1,x]\rvert}{x}=0\]or even (motivated by Behrend's bound)\[\sum_{\substack{x <n\\ x\in A}}\frac{1}{x}\ll \frac{\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}.\]In [Er97c] he offers \$500 for resolving the questions in the main problem statement above.

A positive answer to the second question was provided by Koukoulopoulos, Lamzouri, and Lichtman [KLL25], who proved that we must have\[\sum_{\substack{x <n\\ x\in A}}\frac{1}{x}=o(\log n).\]A formalised negative answer to the first question is available here: there exists such an $A$ for which $\sum_{x\in A}\frac{1}{x\log x}=\infty$.

See also [858].

If $H$ is bipartite with minimum degree $r$ then there exists $\epsilon=\epsilon(H)>0$ such that\[\mathrm{ex}(n;H) \gg n^{2-\frac{1}{r-1}+\epsilon}.\]
#147: [ErSi84][Er93][Er97c]
graph theory | turan number
Conjectured by Erdős and Simonovits [ErSi84]. A probabilistic argument shows that there exists some $\epsilon=\epsilon(H)>0$ such that\[\mathrm{ex}(n;H) \gg n^{2-\frac{2}{r}+\epsilon}.\]This conjecture was disproved by Janzer [Ja23] for even $r\geq 4$. The case $r=3$ was disproved by Janzer [Ja23b], who constructed, for any $\epsilon>0$, a $3$-regular bipartite graph $H$ such that\[\mathrm{ex}(n;H)\ll n^{\frac{4}{3}+\epsilon}.\]In [Ja23] Janzer conjectures that the above lower bound is sharp, in that for any $r\geq 3$ and $\epsilon>0$ there exists an $r$-regular graph $H$ such that\[\mathrm{ex}(n;H) \ll n^{2-\frac{2}{r}+\epsilon}.\]Janzer's result proves this for even $r\geq 4$.

See also [113], [146], and [714].

$500
Let $\alpha\in[0,1/2)$ and $n,t\geq 1$. Let $F^{(t)}(n,\alpha)$ be the smallest $m$ such that we can $2$-colour the edges of the complete $t$-uniform hypergraph on $n$ vertices such that if $X\subseteq [n]$ with $\lvert X\rvert \geq m$ then there are at least $\alpha \binom{\lvert X\rvert}{t}$ many $t$-subsets of $X$ of each colour.

For fixed $n,t$ as we change $\alpha$ from $0$ to $1/2$ does $F^{(t)}(n,\alpha)$ increase continuously or are there jumps? Only one jump?
#161: [Er90b,p.21]
combinatorics | hypergraphs | ramsey theory | discrepancy
For $\alpha=0$ this is the usual Ramsey function.

A conjecture of Erdős, Hajnal, and Rado (see [562]) implies that\[ F^{(t)}(n,0)\asymp \log_{t-1} n\]and results of Erdős and Spencer imply that\[F^{(t)}(n,\alpha) \gg_\alpha (\log n)^{\frac{1}{t-1}}\]for all $\alpha>0$, and a similar upper bound holds for $\alpha$ close to $1/2$.

Erdős said in [Er90b]: 'If I can hazard a guess completely unsupported by evidence, I am afraid that the jump occurs all in one step at $0$. It would be much more interesting if my conjecture would be wrong and perhaps there is some hope for this for $t>3$. I know nothing and offer \$500 to anybody who can clear up this mystery.'

Conlon, Fox, and Sudakov [CFS11] have proved that, for any fixed $\alpha>0$,\[F^{(3)}(n,\alpha) \ll_\alpha \sqrt{\log n}.\]Coupled with the lower bound above, this implies that there is only one jump for fixed $\alpha$ when $t=3$, at $\alpha=0$.

For all $\alpha>0$ it is known that\[F^{(t)}(n,\alpha)\gg_t (\log n)^{c_\alpha}.\]See also [563] for more on the case $t=2$.

What is $\mathrm{ex}_3(n,K_4^3)$? That is, the largest number of $3$-edges which can placed on $n$ vertices so that there exists no $K_4^3$, a set of 4 vertices which is covered by all 4 possible $3$-edges.
#500: [Er61][Er71,p.104][Er74c,p.81][Er81]
graph theory | hypergraphs | turan number
A problem of Turán. Turán observed that dividing the vertices into three equal parts $X_1,X_2,X_3$, and taking the edges to be those triples that either have exactly one vertex in each part or two vertices in $X_i$ and one vertex in $X_{i+1}$ (where $X_4=X_1$) shows that\[\mathrm{ex}_3(n,K_4^3)\geq\left(\frac{5}{9}+o(1)\right)\binom{n}{3}.\]This is probably the truth. The current best upper bound is\[\mathrm{ex}_3(n,K_4^3)\leq 0.5611666\binom{n}{3},\]due to Razborov [Ra10].

See also [712] for the general case.

$500
Given $n$ distinct points $A\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ must there be a point $x\in A$ such that\[\#\{ d(x,y) : y \in A\} \gg n^{1-o(1)}?\]Or even $\gg n/\sqrt{\log n}$?
#604: [Er57][Er61][Er75f,p.99][Er83c][Er85][Er87b,p.169][Er90][Er95][Er97b][Er97c][Er97e][Er97f]
geometry | distances
The pinned distance problem, a stronger form of [89]. The example of an integer grid show that $n/\sqrt{\log n}$ would be best possible.

It may be true that there are $\gg n$ many such points (Hunter has noted in the comments that this trivially follows from the existence of a single point), or that this is true on average - for example, if $d(x)$ counts the number of distinct distances from $x$ then in [Er75f] Erdős conjectured\[\sum_{x\in A}d(x) \gg \frac{n^2}{\sqrt{\log n}},\]where $A\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is any set of $n$ points.

In [Er97e] Erdős offers \$500 for a solution to this problem, but it is unclear whether he intended this for proving the existence of a single such point or for $\gg n$ many such points.

In [Er97e] Erdős wrote that he initially 'overconjectured' and thought that the answer to this problem is the same as for the number of distinct distances between all pairs (see [89]), but this was disproved by Harborth. It could be true that the answers are the same up to an additive factor of $n^{o(1)}$.

The best known bound is\[\gg n^{c-o(1)},\]due to Katz and Tardos [KaTa04], where\[c=\frac{48-14e}{55-16e}=0.864137\cdots.\]

$500
Is it true that, for every bipartite graph $G$, there exists some $\alpha\in [1,2)$ and $c>0$ such that\[\mathrm{ex}(n;G)\sim cn^\alpha?\]Must $\alpha$ be rational?
#713: [ErSi70,p.379][Er74c,p.78][Er75][Er78,p.30][Er81,p.30][ErSi84][Er91]
graph theory | turan number
A problem of Erdős and Simonovits. Erdős sometimes asked this in the weaker version with just\[\mathrm{ex}(n;G)\asymp n^{\alpha}.\]Erdős [Er67d] had initially conjectured that, for any bipartite graph $G$, $\mathrm{ex}(n;G)\sim cn^{\alpha}$ for some constant $c>0$ and $\alpha$ of the shape $1+\frac{1}{k}$ or $2-\frac{1}{k}$ for some integer $k\geq 2$. This was disproved by Erdős and Simonovits [ErSi70].

The analogous statement is not true for hypergraphs, as shown by Frankl and Füredi [FrFu87], who proved that if $G$ is the $5$-uniform hypergraph on $8$ vertices with edges $\{12346,12457,12358\}$ then $\mathrm{ex}(n;G)=o(n^5)$ but $\mathrm{ex}(n;G)\neq O(n^c)$ for any $c<5$.

A simplified proof was given by Füredi and Gerbner [FuGe21], who extended it to a counterexample for all $k\geq 5$. It remains open whether it is true for $k=3$ and $k=4$ (though Füredi and Gerbner conjecture it is not).

See also [571].

#1135: [La85][Er97e,p.537][La16]
number theory | iterated functions
The infamous Collatz conjecture. For a detailed discussion of the history and theory surrounding this problem we refer to the overview by Lagarias [La10].

This is not a problem due to Erdős; it was first devised by Collatz before 1952. Erdős referred to this problem on several occasions as 'hopeless'. As Lagarias [La16] notes, the closest Erdős ever came to working on problems of this nature is the theorem described in the remarks to [1134].

It is often claimed that Erdős offered \$500 for a solution to this problem; this claim originated in a survey article by Lagarias [La85].

Lagarias reported, in personal communication, that this came from a conversation he had with Erdős and Graham around 1983, in which Graham asked Erdős to make an estimate of what value Erdős would put the problem on his prize scale, to which Erdős replied \$500. Therefore, strictly speaking, Erdős never offered \$500 specifically as a prize, but we include this prize value here for comparing those problems which Erdős rated as 'prize problems'.

This is Problem E16 in Guy's collection [Gu04], in which Guy quotes Erdős as saying "Mathematics may not be ready for such problems".
