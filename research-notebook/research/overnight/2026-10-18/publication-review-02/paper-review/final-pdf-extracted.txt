Treating the Tremors We Built into Robots
What a checked execution layer can promise
Mike Helsel
Working draft for technical review; AI assistance disclosed
18 October 2026
Abstract
A smooth reference, an accepted request, and a completed task are diﬀerent guarantees. We formalize
part of their connection for a scalar execution layer built from an established four-stage moving average. Lean
checks a rational ﬁnite history against jerk and acceleration limits at every future real time. A suﬃcient speed
check uses the cubic Bernstein coeﬃcients on each knot cell. A ﬁnite candidate rule preserves committed
motion and can delay or reject a new execution. A commit rule binds the certiﬁcate to the live task, retained
history, and declared timing. We prove that completed event tails can be removed without changing future
motion, and that a checker using adjacent knot cells has the same acceleration decisions. A conditional theorem
carries the physical state from time zero and proves tracking, progress, position arrival, and terminal speed
and acceleration bands for one return task under changing response and disturbance.
A predeclared numerical comparison starts three methods from the same reference and physical state. The
retained ﬁlter has about 0.40 times the squared-jerk integral of a native planner with matched arrival, but
about 2.09 times its extra forward travel. Only the native fast method completes the tighter task. These results
support a checked-contract case study, not a new smoothing algorithm, a general vision-language-action (VLA)
remedy, or a physical robot result. A stronger oﬄine smooth comparator reduces FIR’s jerk integral and travel,
but raises peak jerk and still misses the tighter task. These diﬀerent measures must not be collapsed into one
claim of smoothness.
1 The guarantee has three parts
A planner requests a task goal g. An executor produces a reference q. A controller moves the physical position
x. Their errors satisfy
x − g = (x − q) + (q − g). (1)
A small tracking error x − q can coexist with a failed task. The reference may arrive too late, travel too far after
a reversal, or continue an old goal after rejecting a request.
The case study asks whether a ﬁnite retained history can make these contracts explicit and executable. It does
not infer whether a new request is noise or a real change of intent. The title describes an engineering symptom;
it does not propose a neurological mechanism.
Prior work sets the scope. Finite impulse response (FIR) planning and derivative limits are established.
Biagiotti and Melchiorri already discuss acceleration growth under an overlapping reversal and waiting as a
response [ 1]. Their later work addresses time optimization under kinematic and frequency constraints [ 2]. We
use the reversal as a regression case, not a discovery. Ruckig supplies online jerk-limited motion from nonzero
initial states [ 3]. Reference governors and ModelPlex already connect future constraints, uncertainty, and checked
execution [ 4, 6]. A network reference governor already uses plant-side acceptance checks for delayed or overtaken
commands; its Remark 23 separates constraint satisfaction from tracking an outdated requested reference [ 5].
Timing and request identity are therefore established design concerns. The proposed contribution is the specialized
Lean contract and its reproducible boundaries. Its research value still needs external specialist review.

The ﬁrst acceleration implementation enumerates candidate pairs. A proved replacement takes the minimum
strictly greater knot for each left endpoint. The theorem same_acceptance equates the two Boolean results,
including invalid-input rejection. This optimization changes evaluation work, not the accepted set.
Theorem 2 (Removal of completed tails). Remove every event with τi+4h ≤ c, and add the sum of its increments
to g0. For every t ≥ c, the resulting position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk equal those of the original reference.
Both guard decisions are preserved.
Completed responses have position contribution δi and zero derivative. This result does not preserve past motion
or bound the number of active events. An unbounded arrival rate can still require unbounded active history.
A suﬃcient speed check. On a cell [tL, tR] of length d > 0, the cubic velocity has Bernstein coeﬃcients
b0 = q′(tL), b 1 = q′(tL) + d
3 q′′(tL), b 2 = q′(tR) − d
3 q′′(tR), b 3 = q′(tR).
Its value is a convex combination of these four coeﬃcients. If every coeﬃcient has magnitude at most V , the
speed is at most V throughout the cell. The Lean checker connects this identity to the actual retained reference,
including its ﬁnal zero-speed tail.
This is a suﬃcient check, unlike the exact acceleration check. A proved example has speed at most 1/6 but a
coeﬃcient of 3/16, so it is rejected. Its decision can change when the certiﬁcate partition changes, even when
the motion does not. Zero-size events can reﬁne this speed test, but their nominal tails also aﬀect the timed
selector. Such events are not a proved reﬁnement of the whole selector. The earlier pruning equivalence and
timing measurements apply to the jerk and acceleration checks, not this new guard.
3 Admission can repair a limit and still fail a task
A new increment is relative to the ﬁnal committed target: δ = grequested − (g0 + ∑
i δi). The commit rule below
binds that ﬁnal target to the task goal. An append-only admission rule retains earlier events. Execution cannot
precede admission. The separately proved causal wrapper also orders execution times. Rejection retains the
existing reference. A new event contributes zero through its execution time, so the executed position and its ﬁrst
three derivatives remain unchanged.
For a ﬁnite supplied list of candidate execution times, the timed selector checks τ ≥ c, speed, jerk, acceleration,
and completion of every event by a reference deadline D. A selected candidate therefore preserves the preﬁx,
meets those reference bounds, and reaches the ﬁnal committed target at rest by D. A result of none says only
that all supplied certiﬁcate tests rejected. With a conservative speed check, this does not even imply that each
listed motion violates a limit. The selector is not a complete trajectory planner.
Fixed reversal. Use h = .2 s. A +20 mm event starts at .22 s. A return to zero becomes available at .52 s. An
immediate −20 mm event passes the jerk limit 7.5 m/s3, but its acceleration magnitude reaches 21/32 = .65625
m/s2, above the .5 limit. Trying execution times [.52, .62] s selects .62 for deadline 1.70 s. The retained reference
then has |q′| ≤ 1/15 m/s, |q′′| ≤ .5 m/s2, and |q′′′| ≤ 7.5 m/s3. It reaches zero with zero derivatives at 1.42 s.
The same candidate list returns none for deadline 1.30 s.
These are exact rational Lean ﬁxtures. They show a useful distinction: repairing a reference limit requires time,
and a deadline may not allow it. Waiting while retaining an old motion is not an emergency stop policy.
Bind the certiﬁcate at the future commit. A pending result records the exact base history, position oﬀset,
width and version, together with the task goal, limits, request time, absolute deadline and version. It also carries
the proposed execution time and increment. At one atomic commit, the live base and task must equal those
recorded values. Equal version integers alone are insuﬃcient. Let tr, tf , tc, te be request, completed computation
and veriﬁcation, atomic commit, and execution times. For dispatch allowance d ≥ 0, acceptance also requires
tr ≤ tf ≤ tc, t c + d ≤ te.

The successful-commit theorem transfers the checked speed, acceleration, jerk and reference-deadline conclusions
to the actual appended history and live task. It proves that the ﬁnal target equals the requested goal and the old
reference jet is unchanged through execution. Rejection retains the entire live base. Using the same certiﬁcate
twice is rejected after the ﬁrst commit changes that base.
The moving ﬁxture uses request .52 s, completion and commit .57 s, dispatch allowance .01 s, and execution .62
s. These are exact input values for a formal client, not measured host timing. Late completion, late commit,
changed history, changed oﬀset, a superseded task and duplicate use are rejected. Removing the timing check
changes already executed reference position; removing history or task binding yields a wrong ﬁnal goal.
This theorem assumes one ideal clock and an atomic state check and update. The client does not measure
hardware clocks or prove a real-time implementation. A late result can be rejected even if every result is late;
rejection is not a progress guarantee. The new rule does not make the oﬄine optimizer causal.

6 Proof artifact and practical limits
The expanded portable package binds 63 local source modules to six independently reviewed proof components. Its
replay builds local sources into an empty directory, checks every local declaration for unsafe code and unapproved
axioms, separately checks 22 module kernels, and requires 33 false clients to fail for mathematical reasons. The
replay ﬁxes the source, reviewer, root, kernel and negative-test plans. Public dependency caches remain a declared
trust boundary. The permitted axioms are propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound. An independent
copied-path replay passed the same source, declaration, kernel and false-claim checks.
An oﬄine client reads an exact rational JSON request. It saves the input bytes, emits Lean literals, proposes
a Boolean result by evaluation, then proves that result and its consequence. Acceptance yields the reference
contract; rejection yields only unchanged live state. A separate kernel check follows. The parser, truthful timing
inputs and atomic runtime state are outside the proof. An earlier client could report a changed input ﬁle’s
hash after proving its old contents. The replacement records the digest of the parsed bytes and rejects a saved
input copy that changes during veriﬁcation. Fresh output ﬁles remain trusted local evidence, not authenticated
attestation. The portable artifact records the separate input-tool integration review.

Exploratory shared-host timings put the new 32-event checker near 220 ms, versus about 22.5 s for the earlier
enumerating check on the same ﬁxed inputs. These are Lean –run measurements, not worst-case bounds or
production native timings. They do not establish a 1 kHz implementation. Removing completed tails helps
storage only when active event arrivals are also controlled.
The next practical test needs measured clocks, an execution schedule, a command interface, and a coupled robot
model. Franka is the intended later platform; no robot was used here. Contact, payload variation, joint coupling,
actuator saturation, and sampled actuation need their own assumptions and acceptance tests. The result does
not certify a machine-safety performance level.
Relation to learned and industrial execution. Real-Time Chunking and VLA-RAIL already retain or
combine actions across changing plans [ 7, 8]. The present scalar history is not a full action chunk. Industrial
systems also expose path and timing choices: F ANUC documents timing conﬁguration, while Yaskawa describes
its SMOOTH function [ 12, 13]. Those public interfaces motivate explicit execution contracts. They do not
establish the vendors’ internal algorithms or provide a measured baseline in this study.
Review status and authorship. The proof gates and numerical comparison have independent agent checks.
No external human review, physical validation, public release, or paper submission has occurred. Mike Helsel set
the research question, proposed the execution-layer direction, identiﬁed the timing interpretation, and required
fair comparisons and independent challenge. Codex agents assisted with source review, mathematics, Lean code,
numerical code, critique, ﬁgures, and writing. Aﬃliation, release scope, license, and venue remain owner decisions.
