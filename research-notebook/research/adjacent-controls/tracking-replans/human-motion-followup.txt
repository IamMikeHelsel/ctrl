Human arms on Earth and in weightlessness
Research date: 2026-10-17

The useful idea is continuity of action while conditions change. Human reaching
remains useful in weightlessness, but its timing, posture, and accuracy can change.
The studies below support adaptive control. They do not identify a single neural
trajectory damper that works independently of gravity. [H1, H2]

Five primary studies

H1. Papaxanthis, Pozzo and McIntyre (1998), Arm end-point trajectories under normal
and micro-gravity environments. Acta Astronautica 43, 153–161.
Two cosmonauts made upward and downward arm movements on Earth and in space.
Path curvature and the relative time to peak speed changed in flight. The latter
returned to pre-flight values after 18 days. This is evidence of retained movement
organization and adaptation, with a very small sample. It does not show identical
motion in both environments or establish a universal jerk bound.

H2. Macaluso et al. (2017), Sensorimotor Reorganizations of Arm Kinematics and
Postural Strategy for Functional Whole-Body Reaching Movements in Microgravity.
Frontiers in Physiology 8:821.
Seven people reached toward nearby or distant targets during parabolic flight,
with their feet secured. Mean movement speed was retained. Success was about 95%
in microgravity and 100% on Earth; that difference was not statistically significant.
Braking took a greater fraction of the motion. Large-target endpoints were farther
from the center in microgravity. The authors infer predictive adjustment; they did
not measure a distinct neural smoothing layer. This was brief weightlessness, with
prior exposure before recorded trials. Different sampling rates and filter cutoffs
also limit any claim of equal high-frequency smoothness. It was not a jerk study.
Access: full publisher methods, results, and discussion inspected.
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00821/full

H3. Flash and Hogan (1985), The coordination of arm movements: an experimentally
confirmed mathematical model. Journal of Neuroscience 5, 1688–1703.
A model minimizing the time integral of squared hand jerk predicted several
features of horizontal reaching: near-straight paths, bell-shaped speed, and slower
motion at sharp bends. Jerk is the rate of change of acceleration. The model was
kinematic; it did not model muscle forces or identify a neural implementation.
A successful fit therefore supports a useful description or design objective.
It does not prove that the brain literally solves that optimization on every update.
Access: primary abstract and the indexed first page of the author-hosted PDF.

H4. Burdet et al. (2001), The central nervous system stabilizes unstable dynamics
by learning optimal impedance. Nature 414, 446–449.
People learned to reach through a robot-generated force field that amplified
sideways error. Endpoint stiffness increased mainly in the unstable direction.
Nine people participated; the learning analysis used five initially naive people.
This supports learned, directional resistance to disturbance. It does not reduce
smoothness to passive muscle damping. Stiffness was estimated from the restoring
response 120–180 ms after a perturbation; that interval does not isolate intrinsic
muscle mechanics from neural responses. Nor does the experiment show that raising
stiffness in every direction improves motion.

H5. Crevecoeur et al. (2016), Long-Latency Feedback Coordinates Upper-Limb and
Hand Muscles during Object Manipulation Tasks. eNeuro 3(1), ENEURO.0129-15.2016.
In 16 people, a robot disturbed the arm while participants held an object.
Goal-dependent hand and shoulder muscle responses appeared together at about 60 ms.
This supports coordinated feedback beneath deliberate action selection. It challenges
a simple serial account in which an arm correction must first finish its computation
before grip compensation starts. Prediction remains relevant, but this study does
not identify one complete circuit or a fixed human planning rate.

What transfers to the robot work

The following are engineering hypotheses, not findings about a neural circuit.

1. Keep a motion state between decisions. A new goal should change the future
motion without resetting the current position, velocity, or acceleration. This is
compatible with our finite handoff theorem. It still needs a rule that turns a goal
change into an admissible next segment.

2. Separate command smoothing from physical damping. A filter can make a requested
path smooth. A feedback controller must make the actual tool follow it despite load
and model error. Physical damping concerns energy loss in the motion. These are
related design choices, but one does not establish the other.

3. State precisely which derivative is continuous. For the example filter
  q'' + 2 omega q' + omega^2(q - target) = 0,
retaining q and q' through a target step still allows q'' to jump. A design that
retains acceleration as state, or enforces bounded jerk during the transition,
can address that specific gap. Its bounds, lag, and actuator demands need proof.
Critical damping for a fixed target does not by itself settle arbitrary replans.

4. Let the load model change while the continuity rule stays the same. A candidate
architecture could use the same state-preserving interface on Earth and in orbit,
with different gravity compensation and uncertainty bounds. This is an engineering
separation of responsibilities. It is not evidence that the biological controller
contains such a separate component.

The testable target is: bounded tracking error and bounded changes in motion under
stated target updates, loads, delays, and actuator limits. A comparison should use
the same tasks and plant, then vary the target filter and load estimate separately.
Measure actual tool jerk, tracking error, delay, effort, and stopping reserve.

These five human studies do not establish that every synthetic controller lacks a
trajectory damper. They also do not supply a physical robot model or its parameters.
The analogy is useful for choosing a contract to test and prove. It is not a novelty
claim or a proof that human arms are always smooth.
