Human steadiness: evidence that can guide a robot proof
Research date: 2026-10-17

The useful target is a bounded tool error under stated disturbances, sensing errors,
delays and replans. Human evidence supports several ways to reach that target. It
does not support one cause of tremor, or a method that makes any arm perfectly still.

Eight primary studies guide the engineering work. Two further studies distinguish
pathological tremor from ordinary movement variability. These are experiments and
their limits, not medical advice. None of the proposed robot theorems below has been
proved in this note. Their novelty has not been established.

Evidence map

1. A peak in motion need not be a peak in the command.
Vernooij, Lakie and Reynolds (2015) applied random mechanical inputs to a relaxed
finger and random electrical inputs to its extensor muscle. Both common finger
tremor frequency ranges could be reproduced. Two people without normal afferent
feedback also showed the artificial spectrum. Thus, mechanical resonance can create
these peaks without a rhythmic neural input or intact stretch reflex. This does not
show that neural input never contributes to natural tremor.
Robot lesson: identify the arm, tool and contact resonances before blaming the
planner. Do not infer a neural oscillator from an output spectrum alone.
https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/jn.00519.2014

2. Shared motor input also contributes.
Halliday et al. (1999) recorded finger acceleration, surface EMG and pairs of motor
units in 13 people, with and without added mass. They found load-independent
contributions associated with motor-unit synchrony in 1–12 Hz and 15–30 Hz bands.
Coherence identifies a shared timing relation; it does not, by itself, identify a
unique brain source or establish that this source causes every observed oscillation.
Robot lesson: separate correlated commands, shared sensor errors and physical
coupling. Several sensors seeing the same oscillation are not independent evidence.
https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10217777/

3. Force variability can grow with the requested force.
Jones, Hamilton and Wolpert (2002) compared voluntary thumb contractions with
electrically induced contractions in five people. Force standard deviation grew
roughly linearly with mean force during voluntary contraction, but stayed nearly
constant during stimulation over the tested range. Their motor-unit model showed
that recruitment structure can produce this scaling. The experiment does not prove
that all noise originates in a noisy high-level neural command.
Robot lesson: test whether actuator, friction or estimator errors grow with effort.
A fixed additive-noise bound may be too optimistic at high control effort.
https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/jn.2002.88.3.1533

4. More stiffness can make pointing worse.
Morrison and Newell (2000) asked people to minimize finger motion while increasing
voluntary arm co-contraction. Higher stiffness generally increased the power of
lower-frequency tremor components and increased finger tremor. It also reduced
compensatory motion between limb segments. This is a counterexample to a universal
“stiffen every joint” rule, not a claim that stiffness is always harmful.
Robot lesson: minimize tool error. Do not require every joint to be motionless if
small, coordinated joint motions keep the tool steady.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10900057/

5. Selective stiffness can stabilize an unstable task.
Burdet et al. (2001) used a robotic interface to create an unstable force field during
arm movement. Participants learned to change the magnitude and orientation of arm
impedance to resist the instability. This supports task-specific impedance, rather
than uniform stiffening. The task was controlled planar reaching. It does not prove
stability for arbitrary contacts, loads or delays.
Robot lesson: reserve stiffness for task directions that need it. Certify damping,
effort limits and the effect of changing stiffness as well.
https://www.nature.com/articles/35106566

6. Prediction and impedance solve different parts of the task.
Osu et al. (2003) compared a velocity-dependent stable force field with a divergent,
unstable field. Error patterns and EMG differed between the fields. A rotated
divergent field supported a combination of learned compensation for consistent
forces and impedance for instability. The internal-model explanation is supported
by behavior and EMG; the experiment does not directly read a complete neural model.
Robot lesson: compensate predictable forces, then use feedback and a robust margin
for the remaining error. Replanning alone cannot remove an unstable interaction.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14615431/

7. Arm and grip corrections can be coordinated quickly.
Crevecoeur et al. (2016) tested 16 healthy participants. A robot pushed the arm toward
or away from a goal, including a task where the hand held an instrumented object.
Goal-dependent hand and shoulder muscle responses appeared together at about 60 ms.
This challenges a strictly serial “correct the arm, then compute grip” explanation.
The authors leave the sensory contributions and detailed finger-contact mechanics
open. The result does not establish a fixed human replan interval.
Robot lesson: one fast feedback policy can coordinate arm motion and grip force
beneath the slower planner. Grip feasibility must be checked along with motion.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4785384/

8. Sensor weights depend on direction and precision.
Van Beers, Sittig and Denier van der Gon (1999) tested hand localization with vision,
proprioception, and both, including prism shifts. Ten participants' combined estimates
supported direction-dependent weighting related to the precision of each sense.
This is a perceptual experiment, not a proof that a controller is robust to sensor
bias, drift, correlation or delay.
Robot lesson: use calibrated, direction-dependent state uncertainty. Do not treat
an average or a covariance estimate as a hard error bound without further evidence.
https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/jn.1999.81.3.1355

Why “the neurology of tremor” is not one mechanism

Ordinary physiological motion can contain mechanical resonance, common neural
drive, feedback effects and force variability at the same time. Reflex feedback can
interact with the moving limb, but study 1 shows that an intact reflex is not needed
to reproduce its tested artificial spectra. Different limbs, loads, tasks and
measurement methods can emphasize different components. Studies 1 and 2 therefore
do not license a universal “all mechanical” or “all neural” claim.

Essential tremor and Parkinsonian resting tremor must not be merged into this
account as one failed servo. In a double-blind study of 11 participants with
essential tremor, phase-locked stimulation near the cerebellum changed tremor
amplitude. Effective phases differed between people and days. This supports a role
for timing in that intervention. It does not identify one cause of all essential
tremor; the proposed detailed circuit mechanism also uses modelling.
Schreglmann et al. (2021):
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-20581-7

Helmich et al. (2011) studied 21 tremor-dominant Parkinson patients, 23 without tremor,
and 36 controls, using imaging and EMG. Basal-ganglia activity tracked transitions
into tremor; cerebellothalamic activity tracked amplitude. The paper proposes an
interaction between circuits. Its authors explicitly say that fMRI is correlational
and that a response could be reactive rather than causal. This is a useful warning
against fitting one oscillator and declaring the biological mechanism solved.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21387372/
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ana.22361

Three mathematical mechanisms worth transferring

A. A replan must not spend more physical energy than the controller can dissipate.

Use a local error model M e'' + B e' + K(t)e = w. Here e is tool or joint error,
M is constant positive-definite inertia, B is damping, K is symmetric stiffness,
and w includes model error and reference acceleration effects. Define

  E = (e'^T M e' + e^T K e) / 2.
  E' = -e'^T B e' + e'^T w + e^T K' e / 2.

This identity exposes a gap that a jerk bound cannot close: changing stiffness can
add energy even when position, velocity and acceleration are continuous. At a gain
switch with continuous physical state and unchanged reference,

  E_after - E_before = e^T (K_after - K_before) e / 2.

Candidate certificate: admit a reference and impedance update only when the
controller has enough verified dissipation or stored energy budget to pay for it.
Keep a certified stop/hold continuation available. Smooth reference handoff is one
premise of this result; physical tracking is another. This draws on established
passivity and impedance ideas. No novelty claim follows from the human analogy.

Important proof limit: this plain E identity dissipates velocity, not all position
error. It alone does not prove exponential convergence or a uniform position tube.
Use a suitable cross term/common quadratic storage, or prove the missing argument.
Time-varying inertia and changing contact add terms that also need to be bounded.

B. Certify the residual motion, including every replan.

For z = (e,e'), seek a positive quadratic V and constants alpha > 0, c >= 0 such
that the actual closed-loop model gives V' <= -alpha V + c*delta^2 between updates.
Assume disturbances and state-estimation errors meet the certified bounds. At each
update, require V_after <= V_before + J_i. Then target the bound

  V(t) <= exp(-alpha*t)*V(0)
          + c*delta^2/alpha * (1-exp(-alpha*t))
          + sum over t_i <= t of exp(-alpha*(t-t_i))*J_i.

This makes “steady” measurable. A nonzero disturbance gives a nonzero residual bound.
The sum also shows why arbitrarily many individually small replans can matter.
An update gate can enforce J_i <= 0, or cap their accumulated effect. It should also
track delay, actuator saturation and grip constraints. A minimum wait alone does
not establish the derivative inequality or a valid switching bound.

C. Set gains from task error, noise and resonance; do not maximize them.

A simple robot counterexample is a damped mode
  m*x'' + c*x' + k*x = F*cos(omega*t).
Its steady periodic amplitude is
  |F| / sqrt((k-m*omega^2)^2 + (c*omega)^2).
With fixed positive m, c and omega, raising k toward m*omega^2 increases this
amplitude. Thus higher stiffness can worsen disturbance rejection at a selected
frequency even when the system remains stable. This is an elementary engineering
example, not a fitted model of the experiments above.

Task-space certificates should allow harmless joint motion and constrain the tool
position, orientation and contact force. A pose bound also needs a bound on the
kinematic Jacobian and its variation over the certified region. Flexible tools and
unobserved contact forces need extra state or explicit uncertainty.

What the next experiment should distinguish

Use the same arm and goal with (1) planner switching disabled, (2) scripted bounded
replans, and (3) VLA replans. Inject separately a periodic load, sensor noise, command
delay and a contact change. Compare tool error and force, not joint quietness alone.
Compare fixed minimum-wait smoothing against continuous handoff, an energy gate and
a certified tracking bound. Keep accepted and rejected replans in the record.

This can reveal whether the dominant problem is command switching, resonance,
observation error or contact dynamics. A low-pass filter can reduce one oscillation
while adding lag; its tracking and safety cost must be measured and bounded too.

Evidence boundary

Human studies motivate the design. They do not supply robot parameter bounds or a
Lean proof. A Lean proof would validate the stated mathematical implication. Physical
tests must justify the model and bounds. Unmodelled disturbances, saturation and
contact changes remain outside a certificate unless it covers them explicitly.
