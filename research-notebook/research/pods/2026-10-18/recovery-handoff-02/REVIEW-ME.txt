What this round adds
We closed the first-command delay and measurement-history gap for two fixed
handoff cases. We also built a symbolic stopping bound for braking that returns
within a known time. These are scalar model results. They do not yet establish
a complete stop-and-hold operation or a submission-ready paper.

1. Keep the original clock through handoff — ACCEPTED
The first measurement is already old when its command reaches the drive.
The proof accounts for motion under the pending command, preserves the same
physical state at the switch, and retains the original measurement timestamp.
It derives the safe startup region and later error bound from the dynamics.
It does not assume a safe dispatch state or an ongoing safety invariant.

The first reading is (position, velocity, acceleration)=(.14,-.01,-1.17) in SI
units. Sensor errors are bounded by (.001,.01,.05). The first dispatch delay
is at most 0.5 ms. Total reading age, including time held after dispatch, is
at most 1 ms. Response rate is 8 to 12 per second. Target error is at most
.01 m/s². Feedback is -8(x-c)-6v-a/2, c=.088125 m, clipped to [-2,2] m/s².
Physical target error is added after clipping.

Two separate proofs cover pending commands -1 and -1.21 m/s². They do not
certify every command between those values. The stronger case keeps the
pending effective target in [-1.22,-1.20] m/s². This matches one condition of
the earlier braking proof. Its remaining proof error budget is only
.00005 m/s², compared with .00105 m/s² in the -1 case.

For any supplied execution under the full contract, the axis remains between
32.6 and 143.65 mm at every instant of its supplied finite horizon. The whole
transformed state set is preserved. The speed and acceleration bounds
are 0.4442 m/s and 3.5536 m/s². These establish containment, not precision rest.
General existence and uniqueness remain open. Two exact nonconstant examples
construct delayed executions through 1 ms, with a real command change at 0.5 ms.

The clock does not restart at dispatch. A reading taken at 0 and dispatched
at 0.5 ms has only another 0.5 ms left for its first hold. A 1 ms sample period
plus 0.2 ms latency has peak reading age 1.2 ms and fails this timing condition.
The diagram in figures/total-reading-age.png shows this distinction.

2. Bound travel when effective braking returns — ACCEPTED
The model is x'=v, v'=a, a'=r(q-a). Here q is the effective physical
acceleration target, not just the command sent to a motor. Initially,
x<=X, 0<=v<=v0 and a<=A, with v0,A>=0. Before the recovery deadline T>=0,
q<=A and r>=0. After T, q<=-b and r>=lambda, with b,lambda>0, through the
computed horizon. Rates are continuous within each phase. One continuous
physical state spans the change, and each phase satisfies its stated ODE.

Let V=v0+A*T+(A+b)/lambda. The theorem gives a first zero velocity
by T+V/b and, until then, a forward position bound
  x <= X + v0*T + A*T²/2 + V²/(2*b).
It derives the acceleration envelope before recovery from the ODE. No upper
recovered response-rate bound is needed. A separate lemma retains the full
exponential upper envelope before the conservative simplification above.

This is a supplied-execution theorem. It does not prove permanent rest,
a lower travel limit or later holding. It requires the braking conditions
through the computed horizon, even if velocity reaches zero earlier. A drive
that changes mode at zero needs a separate argument for that change.
Continuity includes acceleration;
impact-like acceleration jumps and instantaneous contact-force loss are outside
this model. Shoe and tire grip motivate the question but are not validated
physical applications of this equation.

The exact coasting counterexample has r=1, q=0, a=0, v=1 and x=t. A positive
response rate alone does not cause braking. The checked nonempty recovery
example coasts with q=0 for one second, then switches to q=-1 with rate 1 and
no state reset. Its finite horizon is three seconds. The independent reviewer accepted
this example and the general conditional statement.

Where recovery could apply
A candidate is a driven axis with a known maximum wait before effective
braking becomes available. The bound could screen clearance, speed or delay
requirements. A temporary physical braking limit is another candidate if its
recovery time and minimum authority can be justified. Neither is validated
hardware here. A shoe or tire losing contact needs an additional mechanical
model; the present continuous-acceleration model does not supply that physics.

What failed, and why it matters
The simple formula gives 160.5859375 mm for the old stopping contract. Our old
sharper certificate already proves a 150 mm limit. The new simple formula
therefore loses this comparison. It can also be very loose near rest, and its
bound can increase with a larger braking floor. This is conservatism from a
dropped exponential term, not evidence that stronger physical braking is worse.
See figures/bound-conservatism.png. Do not replace the sharper old certificate.

The handoff guard also fails to cover the whole old stopping family. Numerical
constant-rate examples at 8, 10 and 12 per second peak at 140.711, 132.525 and
126.761 mm. The fixed handoff admission requires 139 to 141 mm. Two examples
stop short of it. These are numerical diagnostics, not formal impossibility
proofs. They expose a missing connection, not unsafe stopping behavior.
See figures/admission-gap.png and root-admission-reachability-screen.json.

Paper and next decision
Delay, command history, backup control and formal intersample safety are
established topics. The primary-source scope checks are in ROOT-SCOPE-NOTES.txt
and the existing digital-hold-01/grader/SOURCES.txt. No matched performance or
proof-effort comparison was run here. Novelty and practical benefit remain open.

The next useful proof needs ranges for the full starting state and physical
input, including lower position, acceleration and effective-input bounds.
The old upper-travel theorem does not supply those bounds. Derive an admission guard for the stopping
outcomes in that set, then prove that a sampled detector reaches and accepts it.
Define the required holding accuracy, speed, acceleration and settling time.
Then compare the same complete contract against an independent reachability method.
More isolated scalar bounds would not resolve the usefulness concern.
